\section{Engineering Implications and Limits}
A resumable FFT is most attractive when a caller can tolerate delayed analysis,
wants explicit control of work on its existing thread, and needs complete
spectra at a modest frame rate. Whether it is preferable to an optimized batch
FFT depends on the actual callback budget and host scheduling model. If a
batch transform already fits comfortably, the extra state and cadence policy
may provide little practical benefit. For a small set of frequently updated
bins, a sliding formulation can be a more appropriate starting point. For
large analysis tasks, a worker may offer a better throughput/latency tradeoff
when the host permits it, consistent with the alternatives examined by
Battenberg and Avi\v{z}ienis~\cite{battenberg2011}. A comparison should include
an optimized batch FFT on the engine thread and, where supported, a worker
implementation, with equivalent input windows, output processing, and requested
cadence. It should report actual publication delay as well as engine cost.

The first external-backend comparison should include FFTW, Rack's PFFFT, and
vDSP on macOS~\cite{frigo2005,pffft,vdsp}. Plans and working buffers should be
prepared outside timed execution, with setup cost reported separately. An
isolated real-transform comparison and a matched complete-analysis comparison
answer different questions; both should include required data conversion and
label the included work. Proposed tables should report backend configuration,
numerical error, storage, aggregate cost, callback-duration tails, and spectrum
age. Figures should show how cost varies with transform length and how callback
duration trades against publication delay. These are planned comparisons, not
evidence that suspension outperforms an optimized batch transform.

For a visualizer, publication is only one stage of end-to-end latency.
The delay decomposition of Pr\r{u}\v{s}a and Holighaus~\cite{prusa2016}
motivates measuring display consumption and repaint timing separately from
analysis completion. Our exact-hop publication cadence does not guarantee
equally spaced screen updates or bound the age of the spectrum when displayed;
the archived timing campaigns do not measure that path.

A complementary experiment should compare hopping, windowed sliding, and
feedforward methods~\cite{garrido2016,park2014,rafii2018,eleftheriadis2023}
across transform lengths, supported hops, and requested
bin counts. It should retain equivalent windowing, normalization, smoothing,
and output processing, and report storage, memory traffic, aggregate cost, publication delay,
and block-duration tails. A periodic-Hann baseline would permit an exact short
window kernel; truncated kernels should be evaluated separately. Real-input
variants should be included where available.

Van der Byl and Inggs examine error in several sliding DFT structures under
floating- and fixed-point arithmetic~\cite{vanderbyl2016}. Their study motivates
treating numerical accuracy as a separate criterion from stability. Our proposed
evaluation would compare long streams against independently recomputed transforms
in single and double precision, including weak tones beside strong ones and
silence following a strong signal. It should track complex-bin and magnitude
error over time, and phase error where the reference magnitude is appreciable.
These workloads are our proposed methodology, not a reproduction of that study
or results of the archived campaigns.

Sample-call costs should also not be confused with device-buffer deadlines.
If the host renders $D$ samples in one device callback, its relevant demand is
the sum of the associated module work over those $D$ samples, plus other engine
costs. In the original path, a device callback containing preparation and
reconstruction can still experience a substantial burst. A future host study
should vary $D$, relative frame phases, analyzer count, and background patch
load, including concurrent display consumption. Relative phase matters because
several individually tolerable bursts can coincide; Bor\ss{} uses staggered
filter schedules to address the analogous convolution problem~\cite{borss2009}.

Skare's audio benchmark framework separates throughput from median, percentile,
and maximum buffer-processing times, and distinguishes continuous batch
execution from periodic invocation~\cite{skare2024}. Its periodic simulation
also differs from variable plugin invocation times within a running DAW.
Balasubramaniam et al. subsequently evaluate neural audio processing under
controlled plugin contention, recording plugin timing, session callback
utilization, driver-reported underruns, and worker inference underruns as
distinct outcomes~\cite{balasubramaniam2026}. Their virtual audio driver omits
physical-interface behavior. These studies inform our measurement design;
their GPU and inference results do not establish FFT-backend rankings.

A Rack evaluation should accordingly distinguish callback-budget exceedances
from observed audio underruns and report its driver, load, and observation
period. For a worker analyzer, missed requested publications and spectrum age
also matter. Superseded display snapshots are a separate outcome: our mailbox
deliberately permits them when the UI consumes more slowly than the engine
publishes. These are proposed host measurements, not results of the campaigns
reported here.

For that future evaluation, Kalibera and Jones provide a method for allocating
repetitions across builds, executions, and iterations according to the sources
of variation, and for reporting effect-size confidence
intervals~\cite{kalibera2013}. A pilot study should identify which levels matter
on each platform before selecting repetition counts. Their methodology has
not been applied retrospectively to our archived campaigns. Sensitivity to
layout and execution environment should also be investigated, following the
measurement-bias concerns of Mytkowicz et al.~\cite{mytkowicz2009}; identical
rebuilds alone need not sample different layouts. Average-cost intervals and
observed callback maxima answer different questions, and neither provides a
hard worst-case execution-time bound.

Wilhelm et al. distinguish observed execution-time extrema from the true
worst case and from a safe upper bound~\cite{wilhelm2008}. Their survey also
explains why caches, pipelines, and branch prediction complicate timing
analysis. In those terms, our quota proof bounds the number of scheduled work
units per call, while the timing campaigns report observations from sampled
executions. Neither supplies a hardware timing model covering all relevant
execution states, so their combination does not establish a safe execution-time
bound.

The one-hop implementation makes those formerly concentrated passes resumable
and defines cancellation and ownership explicitly. The current fixed weight
for dirty window preparation and Fourier's output reduces their operation
density, but still permits systematic cost differences between stages and
windows. Further calibrated
weights would add machine-dependent assumptions and would not prove worst-case
execution time on a general-purpose OS. The historical measurements above do
not evaluate this newer weighting policy.
Following the transform-subdivision discussion in Wefers~\cite{wefers2015},
another experiment could compare individual butterflies with small batches or
short subtransforms under the same hop and publication-latency constraint.
Coarser units may improve locality and reduce scheduling overhead, but increase
the indivisible work charged to a call. Both effects require measurement;
the present study establishes no preferred granularity beyond its tested design.
The depth-first traversal of B\'ecoulet and Verguet~\cite{becoulet2021}
suggests a related experiment: compare immediate and resumable execution of
each candidate kernel, across working-set sizes and concurrent analyzer counts.
This would separate changes in factorization and locality from the effect of
suspension, while retaining the same output and latency requirements.

The scheduler always completes its work-count quota before advancing its
logical hop. If a host call runs late, it does not silently overwrite an
unfinished frame, but neither does it rescue the audio deadline. Actual host
overruns require measurements beyond this logical schedule.

The principal limitations of the evidence are its single host and compiler,
small number of repeated runs, same-session resampling, absence of an optimized
library baseline, and absence of an end-to-end Rack workload. The numerical
campaign uses independent direct transforms only for small sizes; large-size
checks establish schedule equivalence rather than independent correctness.
The timing studies do not evaluate non-power-of-two lengths, inverse
reconstruction applications, concurrent UI consumption, or calibration accuracy
of every window/display setting. Production regression tests add invalid-setting
rejection and concurrent mailbox stress; they are separate from timing evidence.
