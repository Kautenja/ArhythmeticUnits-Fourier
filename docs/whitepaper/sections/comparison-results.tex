\section{Comparative Results}
\label{sec:comparison-results}
The confirmation experiment supports a scheduling tradeoff, rather than a
throughput advantage for the scalar resumable transform. Distributing the
complete analysis reduces the concentration of work in the tested callbacks,
but optimized native batch implementations generally have lower aggregate
cost and publish the selected frame sooner. The inverse and complete-chain
controls further delimit the result: distributing butterflies alone does not
consistently beat an optimized batch transform's callback duration.
All results below come from the September 30 confirmation campaign. Historical
measurements are not used to rank the current implementations.

\subsection{Scalar analysis: cost, concentration, and age}
Table~\ref{tab:comparison-analysis} uses the middle of the three primary
transform lengths, rather than selecting the length with the largest effect.
The workload fixes the frame cadence, precision, required unsmoothed magnitudes,
instance count, input bytes, and callback origin. At this configuration,
the production core costs \PaperCmpCoreCost\,ns per engine sample and has a
callback p99 of \PaperCmpCoreTail\,$\mu$s. The native vDSP adapter costs
\PaperCmpVdspCost\,ns per sample with p99 \PaperCmpVdspTail\,$\mu$s: the
core has \PaperCmpCostRatio{} times the aggregate cost while reducing this
p99 statistic by a factor of \PaperCmpTailRatio. This is a practical
implementation comparison, not the isolated cost of suspending a common
FFT kernel.

The matched PFFFT scheduled-batch/hybrid pair gives a narrower attribution.
Both retain the same transform arithmetic and surrounding scheduled tasks;
the hybrid redistributes preparation and output work while leaving the native
FFT indivisible. Its smaller p99 shows that distributing those surrounding
stages already removes much of the callback concentration. The production
core lowers p99 further at this configuration, but costs more than the hybrid.
The legacy batch/incremental pair likewise shows reduced concentration,
while its remaining unscheduled frame and output stages explain why it does
not attain the production core's p99. These controls separate the benefit of
work placement from the much larger difference between scalar kernels and
optimized native implementations.

Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-length} retains all three primary lengths at a
fixed hop and callback size. The core's lower callback p99 coexists with
higher aggregate cost than the native analysis adapters throughout these
lengths. Fixed $H$ makes longer transforms more work per publication; these
curves are not an experiment with constant overlap ratio. The hybrid's large
throughput variation at the longest length is retained: its session means
span \PaperCmpHybridLargeLow--\PaperCmpHybridLargeHigh\,ns per sample.
A single favorable run would misrepresent this observation.

Publication delay is the other material cost. At $H=1024$ and 48\,kHz,
the production core and hybrid add \PaperCmpDelayMs\,ms after the selected
frame endpoint; immediate implementations add none. At $N=4096$, the nominal
spectrum-center age is therefore \PaperCmpCoreAgeMs\,ms for the core and
\PaperCmpBatchAgeMs\,ms for immediate batch execution. Neither quantity
includes callback-end visibility, repaint delay, or smoothing response.
A visualizer that can accept the additional age and places high value on
shorter callbacks has a reason to choose the core; an application primarily
constrained by throughput or publication age has a reason to prefer native
batch execution.

Observed maxima do not establish the same ranking as p99. For example,
the core's maximum at $N=2048$ is \PaperCmpCoreSmallMax\,$\mu$s despite a
small session-median p99. The measurement includes interruptions on a general
purpose operating system; these observations are retained, not attributed
solely to the arithmetic scheduler. Conversely, the finite observations
cannot certify that a more concentrated implementation meets every deadline.
The logical credit bound and the measured duration distributions answer
different questions.

\subsection{Inverse jobs and complete frequency-domain chains}
Table~\ref{tab:comparison-synthesis} keeps the periodic inverse job and both
complete chains distinct. Relative to their matching scalar batch controls,
the incremental controls substantially lower callback p99 while leaving
aggregate cost of a similar order. Native implementations change that
conclusion: for the periodic inverse workload at the representative size,
PFFFT and vDSP have both lower aggregate cost and lower p99 than the
incremental control. Thus the forward analyzer's measured advantage in
callback concentration does not generalize automatically to the inverse path.

\input{generated/paper-comparison/tables/synthesis.tex}

The complete identity and FIR chains include retention, transforms, spectral
multiplication, normalization, overlap-save extraction, and output delivery.
Their incremental variants have smaller p99 than the native chains at this
configuration, at a large aggregate-work penalty and an additional hop-minus-one
samples of delivery delay. These controls retain bulk input-buffering and frame-copy passes,
including an $O(N)$ inverse-buffer task charged as one scheduling unit. They demonstrate a
partially distributed frequency-domain operation, not the analyzer's
whole-pipeline work bound or a production audio effect.

Isolated execution reinforces the importance of kernel cost. At $N=4096$,
the normalized scalar IFFT costs \PaperCmpScalarIfftUs\,$\mu$s per transform,
compared with \PaperCmpVdspIfftUs\,$\mu$s for vDSP under the isolated-transform
contract. The isolated contract includes input buffering but excludes the periodic
job's per-sample scheduling and additional canonical-output copies. These
values must not be interpreted as streaming cost per sample. The fixtures
also do not justify choosing an FFT implementation for a three-tap filter,
whose direct evaluation is a different, much smaller algorithmic workload.

\subsection{Sensitivity, channels, and configuration changes}
Table~\ref{tab:comparison-extensions} tests the practical limits of the
scalar analysis result. The p99 ordering depends on the hop, number and
alignment of instances, and work performed around the analyzer. At the short
$N=128$, $H=37$ setting, the native vDSP p99 is smaller than the core's.
Staggering many native analyses across callbacks can also reverse an advantage
seen with synchronized publications. This is an alternative means of reducing
aggregate callback concentration when the application controls frame phases.
The independent small-hop campaign has a different observation window and
is not pooled with the primary campaign.

\input{generated/paper-comparison/tables/extensions.tex}

Live window and smoothing updates give another limit: the hybrid's p99 can
be lower than the production core's, even though the core is lower in the
clean-cache reference case. The configured background DSP brings the total
callback statistics closer because that shared work occupies more of the
interval. Higher sample rate changes the callback budget and sample-index
age without inherently reducing the work in a fixed-length transform.
None of these extensions measures a complete Rack patch or an audio device.

Single-sample callbacks illustrate a percentile failure rather than an
especially favorable scheduler. For $N=2048$, $H=1024$, the core and native
vDSP both have p99 \PaperCmpSingleTail\,$\mu$s, equal to the median empty-timer p99 of
\PaperCmpSingleTimer\,$\mu$s, while their retained maxima are
\PaperCmpSingleCoreMax\,$\mu$s and \PaperCmpSingleVdspMax\,$\mu$s.
One transform release per 1024 samples can fall outside p99. These short,
unpaced experiments retain only 64 hops per process; they cannot identify
a precise rare-event tail from the apparent equality of p99.

Table~\ref{tab:comparison-channels} prevents the scalar result from standing
in for the independent-channel implementation. The four-lane core substantially
reduces aggregate work relative to four scalar cores. At the selected size,
its aggregate cost is also below the measured banks of native float analyses,
while retaining a smaller p99. This is evidence for this channel-parallel
implementation and input layout on the measured host, not a general SIMD
advantage over those libraries. The double-precision rows form their own
comparison and retain the same throughput/concentration tradeoff.

The transition suite checks requests, application boundaries, publication
responses, and every affected output together
(Table~\ref{tab:comparison-transitions}). Across both transition horizons,
\PaperCmpTransitionPublications{} published spectra were audited. Requests
can replace earlier requests, and a final request can remain pending at the
finite horizon; neither outcome is silently converted into a successful
response. The displayed response delay can exceed a stationary hop because
it begins at the request and includes latching and changed-hop scheduling.
Event-window maxima include overlap between adjacent request windows and
ordinary system interference, so subtracting the no-change maximum would
not isolate the cost of a settings change.

\subsection{Numerical and resource evidence}
Every analysis coverage record has its expected publication and bin counts.
Table~\ref{tab:comparison-numerical} reports the largest observed vectorwise
errors separately by precision, including the independent channels. The
recorded float and double maxima satisfy their frozen normwise acceptance
budgets established before confirmation. The nonzero float pointwise
diagnostics remain visible: passing a
spectrum norm does not bound the relative error of every weak bin. The
transition audits also pass; their largest relative $\ell_\infty$ errors are
$\PaperCmpFloatTransitionLinf$ and $\PaperCmpDoubleTransitionLinf$ for float
and double respectively.

\input{generated/paper-comparison/tables/numerical.tex}

Inverse and chain checks use their own analytical/time-domain references
and output-amplitude units. Their largest recorded absolute errors across
all stationary configurations are $\PaperCmpFloatInverseAbs$ and
$\PaperCmpFloatChainAbs$ for float, and $\PaperCmpDoubleInverseAbs$ and
$\PaperCmpDoubleChainAbs$ for double, respectively. These maxima do not
replace the reference-specific acceptance tests or extend a partial isolated
transform check to every possible spectrum.

Storage probes require equally careful scope. At the representative scalar
analysis configuration, the recorded C++ live-heap counters are
\PaperCmpCoreHeapKiB\,KiB for the core and \PaperCmpVdspHeapKiB\,KiB for the
native vDSP adapter. These cover intercepted C++ heap allocations but
exclude unknown native-library storage, stack workspace and allocator
overheads; they cannot establish a total-memory ranking. In particular,
observing no intercepted C++ allocation during execution does not exclude
allocation inside an opaque library. The archived provider plans and
allocation-scope records retain these distinctions.
