\section{Results}
\label{sec:comparison-results}
The new measurements support three findings. Placement substantially changes
the typical burst of a fixed implementation. Native kernels change the cost
of obtaining that reduction. Finally, complete host behavior depends on
synchronization, alignment, and release timing as well as analyzer cost.
Every current performance result below comes from the two spec~014 pilots.

\subsection{Placement and native implementation cost}
Table~\ref{tab:study-baselines} compares the scalar reference shape. Matched
distribution reduces the median per-hop peak from \StudyBatchPeak{} to
\StudyCorePeak\,$\mu$s, a factor of \StudyPlacementRatio, while cost rises
from \StudyBatchCost{} to \StudyCoreCost\,ns per engine sample. Across all
three baseline lengths, all four matched implementation families, and both
sessions, distributed/hybrid execution has the lower session hop-peak summary
in \StudyPlacementSigns{} of \StudyPlacementComparisons{} comparisons.
This is descriptive sign consistency over dependent cells, not a significance
test or a ranking of unrelated kernels.

The PFFFT native hybrid costs \StudyPffftHybridCost\,ns/sample with an
\StudyPffftHybridPeak\,$\mu$s hop peak, compared with
\StudyPffftBatchCost\,ns/sample and \StudyPffftBatchPeak\,$\mu$s for its
matched batch path. vDSP and FFTW supply additional tradeoff points.
Figure~\ref{fig:study-tradeoff} shows every process at this shape: a small
peak does not imply minimum aggregate work, and session variation should remain
visible rather than being folded into one provider score. Continuous
instrumented block cost differs from a separate throughput-loop measurement.
Redistributing segments changes dispatch, vector-call sizes and locality;
matched arithmetic alone does not force identical elapsed cost.

The scalar reference is still far from exhausting its 1,333\,$\mu$s block
budget. Its contribution is to isolate work placement. Practical significance
must be assessed at the complete-module and host boundaries below. Callback
p99 and retained maxima also differ markedly from the median hop peak. The
per-hop statistic ensures sparse frame work remains represented, while maxima
retain interruptions and other excursions; none is a safe execution-time bound.

\subsection{Completion horizon and four-channel analysis}
The native horizon sweep makes publication age an explicit design variable
(Table~\ref{tab:study-horizons}). At the demanding controlled shape
$N=16384,H=240$, the four-channel vDSP hybrid's median hop peak increases from
\StudyHorizonFullPeak{} to \StudyHorizonHalfPeak{} to
\StudyHorizonQuarterPeak\,$\mu$s as the horizon contracts from $H$ to $H/2$
and $H/4$. Endpoint age falls from 4.98 to 2.48 to 1.23\,ms. The native FFT
remains indivisible, so a shorter horizon cannot distribute that call further.
The behavior at small sizes and for other kernels need not be monotonic;
all 39 granularity cells remain in the accompanying tables.

At the same four-channel shape, the SIMD production core has a
\StudySimdExtremePeak\,$\mu$s peak at \StudySimdExtremeCost\,ns/sample,
compared with \StudyHorizonFullPeak\,$\mu$s at
\StudyHorizonFullCost\,ns/sample for the full-hop vDSP hybrid. This is a
case favoring the native hybrid on both recorded cost and typical peak.
It changes the earlier manuscript's implication that channel SIMD itself
establishes an advantage over native libraries. The comparison now includes
batched native channels, and the conclusion depends on shape and kernel.
These controlled analysis rows omit module conditioning and display conversion.

\subsection{Shipped defaults and demanding module settings}
Table~\ref{tab:study-modules} measures the actual module calls with paced
releases. Fourier defaults have a \StudyModuleDefaultPeak\,$\mu$s hop peak,
\StudyModuleDefaultBudgetPercent\% of the block budget. At $N=16384$ and
a 5\,ms hop, the complete four-port module reaches
\StudyModuleExtremePeak\,$\mu$s, or \StudyModuleExtremeBudgetPercent\%.
Adding both configured smoothing modes raises that summary to
\StudyModuleBothPeak\,$\mu$s (\StudyModuleBothBudgetPercent\%), with
\StudyModuleBothComputeMisses{} compute-budget exceedances and
\StudyModuleBothMisses{} late releases in 3,840 blocks. Thus the hardest
shipped setting is materially different from the scalar reference or defaults.

Changing to one active port does not turn Fourier into a scalar transform:
its four-lane analysis still runs. Extra voices are summed into each port's
lane rather than becoming independent FFT channels. The table therefore
measures control/input costs without implying a fourfold reduction for three
unpatched ports. No paced native complete-module comparison was collected at
the extreme shape; its continuous native core results cannot fill that gap.
The settings demonstrate a limitation of the production path, not evidence
that an alternative would eliminate its missed releases.

\subsection{Actual Rack engine, alignment, and missed releases}
Four aligned Fourier modules on one engine thread create a substantial
concentration difference (Table~\ref{tab:study-host}). Production's
\StudyHostCorePeak\,$\mu$s typical hop peak uses
\StudyHostCoreBudgetPercent\% of the budget, compared with
\StudyHostBatchPeak\,$\mu$s (\StudyHostBatchBudgetPercent\%) for vDSP batch
and \StudyHostHybridPeak\,$\mu$s (\StudyHostHybridBudgetPercent\%) for its
hybrid. The price is aggregate block wall cost: \StudyHostCoreCost{} versus
\StudyHostBatchCost{} and \StudyHostHybridCost\,ns/sample, respectively.
This is a regime where burst reduction leaves a substantial fraction of the
block available for other work, rather than saving a few microseconds in an
otherwise empty callback.

\input{generated/paper-study/host.tex}

Four engine threads change the relationship. With four aligned modules the
three paths converge near a 100--115\,$\mu$s typical hop peak
(Figure~\ref{fig:study-host}). The empty four-thread engine also has a much
larger callback p99 than the one-thread control. The source's per-sample
barriers and worker dispatch provide a plausible mechanism, but these runs
do not isolate that cost from CPU placement or power-state effects.
The barrier hypothesis motivates host measurement; it is not established
merely by citing the synchronization code. Table~\ref{tab:study-host-accounting}
shows why: the empty four-thread engine consumes about
\StudyEmptyCpuCores{} CPU-seconds per second of nominal audio, consistent
with active worker spinning between releases. Its process CPU remains near
that level with analyzers present. The one-thread engine can sleep between
releases; four threads therefore change the activity and potential power-state
regime as well as parallelism. This is the observed behavior of this pinned
headless engine, not an isolated measurement of barrier overhead.

With 16 aligned modules and four threads, production's typical peak is
\StudyHostManyCorePeak\,$\mu$s versus \StudyHostManyBatchPeak\,$\mu$s for
vDSP batch. Staggering the native instances lowers that peak to
\StudyHostStaggerBatchPeak\,$\mu$s. Staggering is therefore a practical
alternative when frame phases can be chosen. The predeclared 0/16/64-background
sweep with four staggered analyzers did not drive a repeatable saturation
boundary; its latency summaries are not monotonic in filler count. We retain
those cells without presenting filler count as calibrated CPU utilization.

The release-miss counts impose a stronger qualification. The one-thread,
four-module production and vDSP batch rows have \StudyHostCoreMisses{} and
\StudyHostBatchMisses{} late releases, despite very different typical peaks;
the hybrid has \StudyHostHybridMisses. The corresponding FFTW batch row has
\StudyHostFftwBatchMisses, but this provider-specific outcome does not establish
a general scheduler benefit. For 16 aligned modules production has
\StudyHostManyCoreMisses{} late releases while vDSP batch has none in these
runs. Empty-engine controls also miss releases. These are retained observations
under synthetic pacing, with wake delay and backlog included. The data support
headroom and concentration comparisons; they do not establish fewer audio
underruns or a stable rare-event reliability ordering.

\subsection{Consumer age and numerical limits}
Table~\ref{tab:study-consumer} measures the headless consumer. At 60\,Hz,
production and the full-hop vDSP hybrid have median endpoint-age bounds near
38--40\,ms, versus 8--10\,ms for native batch. The difference is approximately
one 30\,ms hop, consistent with the publication contract. At 30\,Hz or under
polling stalls, some complete Fourier snapshots are superseded before
consumption. Correct ownership preserves held data; it does not guarantee
that a consumer sees every produced frame. Logical age bounds do not measure
screen refresh, perceptual acceptability, or real-time freshness during backlog.

All numerical replays pass their declared policies
(Table~\ref{tab:study-numerical}), including non-power-of-two hops, high overlap,
weak/decaying input, silence, and complete-module lifecycle changes. Preparation
had exposed premature magnitude underflow in naive squared norms; the measured
source uses scaled magnitudes for both production and native paths. Long decay
still reaches the explicitly flagged FTZ/display tail. Its observed relative
error can reach one, so the ordinary relative budgets must not be extended to
that region. Retaining the tail and its absolute diagnostics gives a more
informative result than labeling the entire decay uniformly accurate.

Lifecycle replay checks freeze/resume, window and band changes, geometry,
reset, and sample-rate changes separately from stationary rankings. Those
mutations occur between engine blocks; their durations and allocation probes
are retained outside ordinary block intervals. The study therefore verifies
serialized lifecycle behavior without claiming that arbitrary concurrent
control changes or all host callbacks are allocation-free.
